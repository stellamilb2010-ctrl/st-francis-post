From calm to chaos: The Psychology and Neuroscience of Test Anxiety

By Daira Motwani · October 1, 2026

You walk into the classroom, it's 8:00 in the morning, and all the information from last night feels fresh in your mind. No worries, just the need of passing or getting a 7. What you study feels enough and you convince yourself that you are more than ready. Then, the paper is placed in front of you. A simple action of turning the paper and reading the first question results in your heart beating fast and your hands feeling different. All of the sudden you can't remember a single thing you studied. This experience is more than being nervous, this represents a complex psychological response in which our thoughts, emotions and physiological reactions interact with each other.

Test anxiety is defined as a common type of performance anxiety among students, characterized by excessive worry, tension, and physical symptoms that can impair academic performance. While some nervousness is normal, heightened anxiety can interfere with concentration, memory and test taking abilities. Test anxiety can manifest in a variety of ways, and no two students are impacted the same way. These symptoms can stem from various factors, including fear of failure, lack of preparation or high pressure environments.

To be able to understand test anxiety, it is important to connect psychology with neuroscience, since the way we think and feel is connected to our nervous system. Think about a scenario where you are seated in a classroom environment and you feel absolutely safe from any harm, but then you begin worrying about an upcoming examination. Your heart starts racing, your hands become tight and you feel nervous. Even if there is no physical danger present in the surrounding environment, the brain can react to a situation as something that needs to be protected. The brain does not just react to the physical stimuli, but reacts to the way we perceive things around us, causing the brain to think that the nervousness before the test or during it is a threatening situation.

When the brain senses a threatening situation, the amygdala (the part of the brain that detects danger) is activated and releases stress hormones to keep us safe, activating the fight, flight or freeze response in order to give us the best chance of survival. As a result, the other parts of the brain such as the prefrontal cortex (PFC) begin to shut down. The prefrontal cortex is important since it helps us to make decisions and problem solve, all of which students need to be able to answer questions in the exam. When it is shut down it may lead to negative behaviour, which is very bad in exam situations.

An example of this might be when a student comes across a question they don't know or when they start overthinking during or before an exam. The amygdala interprets this as a threat and the body goes into an emergency response. Stress hormones are released, the PFC functions are inhibited, and now the students can't think straight. The anxiety and mental paralysis the student feels at this moment can cause negative behaviour such as not completing the exam.

That's why a “but I knew everything, how did I fail?” is not about you being dumb or lacking knowledge or ability, it's all about test anxiety. It is about getting excessively worried, affecting concentration and leading to potential panic attacks. You may still know the answer, but your brain can't process it due to the high level of stress released.
